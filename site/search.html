The search

Where, how, and when the search actually happens

The looking is measurable too, not just the hiring. Here is where it starts, how it runs, and when the ads are thin.

Weekline · Record · Reviewed 3 November 2026

If a remote search feels slower or more confusing than it should, that’s a reasonable reaction to how the market actually works right now, not a sign you’re searching wrong. The data below explains why.

In short

  • Searches still start on the large boards, then LinkedIn, Google, and employer sites.
  • A search tends to run the same handful of steps, in roughly the same order, whether the goal is a title or just extra cash.
  • Remote postings are roughly a tenth of ads, or less, and about a third of applications, and the share moves by season and by field.
  • An unnamed remote offer that asks for a fee matches a pattern in Federal Trade Commission reports.

Where the looking starts

iHire’s 2025 survey of 1,421 U.S. job seekers asked which resources they use. People could choose more than one. General job boards were 78.5%, LinkedIn 54.9%, niche boards 53.8%, company websites 45.0%, and search engines 41.6%. Networking was 32.7%, social media 15.7%, associations 15.4%, job fairs 12.7%, Craigslist 8.3%, and generative tools 6.9%. The prose summary of that survey rounded the board figure differently. The chart figure used here is 78.5%.

HireClix asked 1,000 adults in 2025 where a search starts. Indeed was 56%, LinkedIn 52% (up from 40% in 2023), Google 41%, and corporate career sites 40%. Fifty-seven percent said reviews on Glassdoor or Indeed affect the decision to apply. One survey asks about every channel used. The other asks about the first stop. Both can be right at once.

A practical search for a professional role tends to be Indeed or LinkedIn, then the employer’s own page, then a review site. A practical search for hourly, local, or shift work still uses Indeed, and adds Facebook groups, Craigslist, and sometimes a staffing agency. Niche boards matter once the field is known: USAJobs for federal roles, Dice or Wellfound for some technology roles, and paid remote boards such as FlexJobs for people willing to pay to filter. Social media is more of a research step than an application step; HireClix found more than half of Gen Z and millennial candidates check a company’s social accounts before applying.

On the employer side of the same iHire survey, 529 respondents named referrals at 71.3%, the career page at 49.5%, LinkedIn at 46.1%, and social at 43.5%. A large share of hiring never appears as a clean public ad at all.

How a search tends to run

  1. Search a title plus “remote,” a city, or “part time.” Salary and work arrangement are the filters that decide whether an ad even gets opened.
  2. Skip the ad if the pay, the employer name, or the location type is missing. iHire found a missing salary was an instant turnoff for 60.7% of seekers, and an unnamed employer for 50.9%.
  3. Check the company site and a review site such as Glassdoor before spending time on the application form.
  4. Apply where the form is short. 51.7% of seekers in the same survey said 10 to 15 minutes is the right length for an online application.
  5. Keep a second path for extra cash that isn’t a job ad at all: marketplace selling, delivery apps, task platforms, or a direct message from someone already known.

Competition on the big boards is high and the reply rate is low once an application is sent. Huntr’s 2025 report, based on about 599,000 applications tracked in its own product (a self-selected set of users, not a population survey), found interview response rates of about 4.5% on Indeed, 3.1% on LinkedIn, and 2.8% on ZipRecruiter, with a higher 11.3% on the smaller, more curated Google for Jobs. Ghosting after applying was the single most common frustration named in the iHire survey, at 59.0%.

When the ads are thin, and when they loosen up

Indeed’s own count of U.S. postings mentioning remote or hybrid work, or listing the location as remote, moved like this: 2.6% in January 2019, a peak of 10.4% in February 2022, 7.8% on 31 October 2024, and 8.3% on 31 January 2025. A secondary compilation of the same tracker puts the share at 8.4% on 31 August 2026, against 8.2% a year earlier, and says it has stayed between about 7.7% and 9.1% since the start of 2024. The all-jobs average hides large differences by field: in the same compilation, remote or hybrid mentions were 39.7% of insurance postings, 32.9% of legal, 32.4% of mathematical, 30.8% of software, 25.2% of marketing, and 20.8% of human resources, against 3.9% for construction, 1.9% for retail, and 1.2% for driving.

Demand runs well ahead of that thin supply. A LinkedIn spokesperson told Business Insider that in September 2025 about 8% of paid U.S. postings offered remote work, and those postings drew 35% of applications; in July 2025 it was about 9% of postings and 37% of applications. A remote posting on LinkedIn draws on the order of four times the applications its share of the board would predict. Robert Half’s professional-job tracker described fully on-site roles rising from 65% of postings it tracks in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 87% in the second quarter of 2026, with 10% hybrid and 3% fully remote; 36% of employers in that survey said they had raised required on-site days over the prior year.

The wider labor market this is happening in is not booming. BLS, seasonally adjusted, September 2026: unemployment 4.2% (7.1 million people), labor-force participation 61.8%, employment-population ratio 59.2%, payrolls up 29,000 that month. Indeed’s Job Postings Index fell from 111.7 on 1 January 2025 to 101.7 at the end of October 2025, just above the pre-pandemic baseline. Searching for remote work now means applying into a cooler market, onto a smaller set of ads, than in 2021 or 2022. Federal civilian remote work is its own special case: Gallup reported hybrid among federal employees fell from 61% in late 2024 to 28% after the 2025 federal return-to-office, with 46% fully on site.

Employers have pulled back on formal flexibility benefits too, without dropping them. SHRM’s 2026 survey found 57% of employers offer hybrid work (down 3 points from 2025) and 45% offer flextime during core hours (down 6 points), while 10% now offer a four-day week (up 2 points).

What the crowded ads have stated

Entry-level remote titles in the FlexJobs database from 1 January to 1 May 2026 were led by customer service, administration, and sales. Named customer-service ads reviewed in autumn 2026, including Concentrix, TTEC, and Firstsource, clustered around $15 to $17 an hour, with an assigned shift, a high-school diploma, and prior customer-service experience. Some required a wired connection rather than a phone hotspot. Those ads expire. They show the shape of the shelf. They are not openings from Weekline.

The signs when an offer isn’t real

The Federal Trade Commission’s 10 March 2025 release said reports of job and employment-agency scams tripled from 2020 to 2024, and reported losses rose from $90 million to $501 million. A September 2025 FTC note put 2024 job-scam reports above 105,000 and losses above $513 million. Task scams were about 20,000 reports in the first half of 2024, against about 5,000 in all of 2023. The patterns in FTC consumer guidance are an unexpected text or chat, a remote title with no duties, any fee, a check the person is told to deposit and partly send back, and pay for liking or rating things followed by a demand to deposit money. Reports go to ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
