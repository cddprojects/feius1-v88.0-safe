Before you say yes

Four signs an offer isn’t legitimate.

Reported job-scam losses topped $500 million in 2024. These four patterns are the ones regulators cite most often — not a reason to doubt every offer, just the signals worth knowing before you accept one.

🚩

No employer name

A vague or unnamed company is the single most common red flag identified in both seeker surveys and FTC fraud reports.

🚩

Any payment required upfront

A kit fee, a training fee, or a deposit before you’ve started work is never legitimate, regardless of how it’s presented.

🚩

A check you’re asked to deposit

Being asked to deposit a check and wire part of it back is the single most common mechanism the FTC tracks in job scams.

🚩

Pressure to decide immediately

A legitimate offer holds up overnight. Pressure to accept on the spot is a tactic, not a feature of real hiring.

Quick answers

What people ask before they get started.

These are the questions nearly everyone has at some point, even if they rarely get asked out loud. Each one has a short answer here and a fuller explanation, with sources, in the guides.

See every note →

Do I need to pay anything to apply?

No. A legitimate employer never charges an application fee, never sells a “starter kit” or training, and never asks you to deposit a check and send part of it back. Any of those is one of the clearest signs of a scam.

Will I need specific equipment to work from home?

Often, yes — a computer that meets a minimum specification and a reliable internet connection, sometimes a wired connection rather than a mobile hotspot. A legitimate employer ships or reimburses the required equipment; it doesn’t ask you to buy it upfront.

Am I classified as an employee or a contractor?

Check which tax form you’re asked to complete. A W-4 means you’re a W-2 employee with taxes withheld from each paycheck. A W-9 means you’re a 1099 contractor responsible for your own taxes.

How long should an application actually take?

About half of job seekers consider 10 to 15 minutes reasonable for an online form. If it’s taking considerably longer, or requiring you to retype a résumé you already uploaded, that’s a documented frustration with the process — not an indication you’re doing something wrong.
