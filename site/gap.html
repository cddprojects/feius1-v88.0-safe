The gap

A thin shelf of ads, a thick pile of applications

The looking and the hiring are both measurable. They do not describe the same volume.

Weekline · Record · Reviewed 3 November 2026

In short

  • Searches still start on the large boards, then LinkedIn, Google, and employer sites.
  • Remote postings are roughly a tenth of ads, or less, and about a third of applications.
  • Insurance, law, math, and software mention remote or hybrid work far more often than retail, driving, or construction.
  • An unnamed remote offer that asks for a fee matches a pattern in Federal Trade Commission reports.

Where the looking starts

iHire’s 2025 survey of 1,421 U.S. job seekers asked which resources they use. People could choose more than one. General job boards were 78.5%, LinkedIn 54.9%, niche boards 53.8%, company websites 45.0%, and search engines 41.6%. Networking was 32.7%, social media 15.7%, associations 15.4%, job fairs 12.7%, Craigslist 8.3%, and generative tools 6.9%. The prose summary of that survey rounded the board figure differently. The chart figure used here is 78.5%.

HireClix asked 1,000 adults in 2025 where a search starts. Indeed was 56%, LinkedIn 52% (up from 40% in 2023), Google 41%, and corporate career sites 40%. Fifty-seven percent said reviews on Glassdoor or Indeed affect the decision to apply. One survey asks about every channel. The other asks about the first stop. Both can be right.

On the employer side of the iHire survey, 529 respondents named referrals at 71.3%, the career page at 49.5%, LinkedIn at 46.1%, and social at 43.5%.

The shelf, by occupation

A secondary compilation of Indeed’s remote tracker, through 31 August 2026, put remote or hybrid mentions at 39.7% of insurance postings, 32.9% of legal, 32.4% of mathematical, 30.8% of software, 25.2% of marketing, and 20.8% of human resources. Construction was 3.9%, retail 1.9%, and driving 1.2%. Robert Half’s second-quarter 2026 professional tracker, from the same secondary source, was 87% on site, 10% hybrid, and 3% fully remote, for professional titles only.

Huntr’s 2025 file of roughly 599,000 tracked applications is a self-selected set, not a national placement rate. Response rates in that file were about 4.5% on Indeed, 3.1% on LinkedIn, 2.8% on ZipRecruiter, and 11.3% on Google Jobs.

What the crowded ads have stated

Entry-level remote titles in the FlexJobs database from 1 January to 1 May 2026 were led by customer service, administration, and sales. Named customer-service ads reviewed in autumn 2026, including Concentrix, TTEC, and Firstsource, clustered around $15 to $17 an hour, with an assigned shift, a high-school diploma, and prior customer-service experience. Some required a wired connection rather than a phone hotspot. Those ads expire. They show the shape of the shelf. They are not openings from Weekline.

In the iHire survey, 60.5% said a hiring timeline would make them more likely to apply, and 57.1% said a salary range would. An unnamed employer was an instant turnoff for 50.9%, and missing pay was an instant turnoff for 60.7%. Ghosting was the most common frustration, at 59.0%. Fake or already-filled postings were 39.3% in the findings chart. Finding remote work was named as a challenge by 24.0%.

The fraud pattern in the public reports

The Federal Trade Commission’s 10 March 2025 release said reports of job and employment-agency scams tripled from 2020 to 2024, and reported losses rose from $90 million to $501 million. A September 2025 FTC note put 2024 job-scam reports above 105,000 and losses above $513 million. Task scams were about 20,000 reports in the first half of 2024, against about 5,000 in all of 2023. The patterns in FTC consumer guidance are an unexpected text or chat, a remote title with no duties, any fee, a check the person is told to deposit and partly send back, and pay for liking or rating things followed by a demand to deposit money. Reports go to ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
