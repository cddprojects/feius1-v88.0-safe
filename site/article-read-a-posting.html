Reading a posting

How to read a remote or flexible posting

Job Compass · General information · Last reviewed 2 November 2026

A useful posting names who is hiring, where the work happens, when it happens, and what it pays.

The short version

  • The employer’s name is part of the posting.
  • Pay is part of the posting in a growing list of states, and readers look for it everywhere.
  • Home-based phone work often still has an assigned shift.
  • A fee to get the job, or a check to cash and partly return, is a known fraud pattern.

What seekers say they need

In the same iHire survey, 60.5% said a hiring timeline would make them more likely to apply, and 57.1% said a salary range would. An unnamed employer was an instant turnoff for 50.9%, and missing pay was an instant turnoff for 60.7%. About half, 51.7%, thought 10 to 15 minutes was the right length for an application. Hearing nothing back was the most common frustration, at 59.0%. Fake or already-filled postings were next, at 39.3% in the findings chart.

Place and hours, written out

“Remote” can mean every state, or one state, or a radius around one city. “Flexible” can mean the person picks the hours, or that the employer will assign a shift and the person works it from home. Published customer-service ads in autumn 2026 from named employers such as Concentrix, TTEC, and Firstsource showed rates around $15 to $17 an hour, with assigned shifts, a high-school diploma, and some prior customer-service experience. Those ads expire. They illustrate the arrangement. They are not openings from this site.

Pay on the posting

A compilation checked against official pages as of 13 October 2026 found pay-range rules in 14 states and the District of Columbia, including California, Colorado, Illinois, New York, and Washington. Thresholds differ by employer size and by whether the job is done in the state. The list is general information, not legal advice. Readers outside those states still treat a missing range as a reason to pause: that is what the iHire result describes.

Patterns the Federal Trade Commission describes

Reported losses to job and employment-agency scams rose from $90 million in 2020 to $501 million in 2024, and reports tripled, according to the FTC’s 10 March 2025 release. A September 2025 FTC note put 2024 job-scam reports above 105,000 and reported losses above $513 million. The patterns in FTC consumer guidance are an unexpected text or chat, a remote title with no duties, any fee to get paid, a check the person is told to deposit and partly send back, and pay for liking or rating things followed by a demand to deposit money.

iHire, State of Online Recruiting 2025. LAYIQ compilation of official pay-transparency pages, 13 October 2026. FTC press release, 10 March 2025, and FTC business blog, September 2025. FTC consumer guidance on job scams.
