Apply

How the application runs, and what it actually asks for

Not what the job is. What filling out the form, and then saying yes to it, actually involves.

Weekline · Companion · Reviewed 3 November 2026

None of what follows is specialized knowledge. It’s just rarely written down in one place, which is why it can feel more intimidating than it actually is.

In short

  • Most online applications follow the same shape: a short form, a few screening questions, sometimes a skills test, then an interview.
  • About half of job seekers consider 10 to 15 minutes the acceptable length for that form.
  • Nearly every U.S. employer asks for the same handful of things before a first day: proof you can legally work, a working setup, and the right tax paperwork.
  • A fee, a kit charge, or a check you’re asked to deposit and partly send back is never a legitimate requirement.

How the application usually runs

An online application for a remote, hourly, or flexible role tends to move through the same handful of stages. First, a form: contact details, a résumé upload, and often a set of “knockout” questions, such as availability, location, or whether you can legally work in the U.S. These questions can end the application immediately if the answer doesn’t fit, which is a common source of silence rather than rejection. Behind that form, most mid-size and large employers run the answers through applicant-tracking software that scores how closely the résumé’s wording matches the posting before any person reads it — which is why two readers with similar experience can get very different results from the same ad, and why it helps to use the posting’s own words for your skills rather than a synonym. iHire’s 2025 survey of job seekers found that re-typing information already on an uploaded résumé was a specific, named complaint, and that about half of seekers think 10 to 15 minutes is the right length for the whole form.

Some roles add a short skills check before a human ever looks at the application: a typing-speed test for data entry or transcription, a short writing sample for content roles, or a basic software quiz for bookkeeping or support roles. If the application clears the screening questions and any skills check, the next step is usually a phone or video interview, sometimes two rounds for office-based professional roles and one short call for hourly or entry-level remote roles. Interview response rates, in one applicant-tracking product’s 2025 data covering roughly 599,000 applications, ran about 4.5% on Indeed, 3.1% on LinkedIn, and 2.8% on ZipRecruiter — a reminder that most applications end without a reply at all, which is a volume problem more than a sign that the application itself was weak.

After an offer, the paperwork starts: the specific documents are covered below, but the shape is the same almost everywhere — sign an offer or contract, complete tax and eligibility forms, and, for jobs that provide equipment, wait for it to ship or submit a reimbursement request rather than pay for it upfront.

How AI has changed the interview itself

By 2026, an AI interview is common rather than rare: Greenhouse’s 2026 survey found 63% of candidates who reached the interview stage had been interviewed by AI, up 13 points in six months. The format varies — a pre-recorded video scored by AI with no person present, a chatbot screening call, or a real-time interview with an AI interviewer — but the common thread is that a person may not review the result before a candidate is moved forward or rejected.

Disclosure is the weak point. In the same survey, 70% of candidates said they were never clearly told in advance that AI would evaluate them, and 21% only found out once the interview had already started. Separately, Enhancv’s 2026 survey found 50.5% of job seekers had been rejected at least once in the past year without any human contact, and 68.5% said AI’s role in that decision was never disclosed to them at all. Most candidates are not against AI being used — Greenhouse found only 19% want less of it — but a large majority want upfront notice, a clear explanation of what is being measured, and a human reviewing the result before a final decision.

None of this changes what the underlying requirements below actually are. It mainly changes who, or what, reviews the application first.

What they’re commonly asked to have

A U.S. job application, online or on paper, asks for the same core information almost everywhere. None of it is unusual to be asked for, and none of it should catch you off guard. Having it ready before starting just saves having to stop mid-form to look something up:

Personal details

  • Name, address, phone, email
  • Whether you’re eligible to work in the U.S.
  • Whether you’re over 18, or have a work permit if not

Education & skills

  • Schools attended, degrees, certificates, graduation dates
  • Licenses or certifications relevant to the role

Work history

  • Past employers, addresses, and supervisor names
  • Start and end dates, and the reason you left each

References

  • 2 to 3 people, with a phone number or email for each
  • Ask them first; some employers ask permission before contacting a current one

Proof you can legally work in the United States. Every W-2 employer must complete Form I-9 within three days of a new hire’s start date, which means presenting an acceptable document such as a U.S. passport, or a driver’s license plus a Social Security card, to confirm identity and work eligibility. This applies to remote hires exactly as it applies to on-site ones; a remote job does not skip it.

The right tax form for how you’re paid. A W-2 employee completes Form W-4, which sets how much income tax is withheld from each paycheck. An independent contractor paid as 1099 completes Form W-9 instead, and no tax is withheld; the contractor sets aside their own income tax and both halves of Social Security and Medicare. Which form you’re asked for is a quick, reliable signal of which kind of work you’ve actually agreed to, regardless of what the posting called it.

A working setup. Home-based customer-service and data roles commonly specify a computer meeting minimum specs, a quiet workspace, and a reliable internet connection, and many postings require a wired connection rather than a phone hotspot or satellite internet. Some roles additionally require a webcam for video calls or supervision software installed on the work computer; this should be stated in the posting or the offer, not discovered after accepting.

Skills proof, where the job calls for it. Typing speed for data entry and transcription, a graded writing sample for content or copy roles, a basic accounting or spreadsheet test for bookkeeping, and a relevant certificate or license for occupations such as medical records or tutoring in a specific subject. Entry-level customer service, administrative, and sales roles — the largest single slice of entry-level remote postings — usually ask only for a high school diploma and some prior customer-service experience.

Sometimes a background check, or more. Roles handling money, health information, or vulnerable people, including many bookkeeping, healthcare-adjacent, and tutoring-of-minors roles, commonly run a background check after a conditional offer, and some add a credit check, a basic cognitive or personality assessment, or a drug or medical screening depending on the field and the state. These are standard, and under the Fair Credit Reporting Act a background or credit check requires your written consent before it happens.

Nothing you pay for. None of the above should ever cost the applicant money. A legitimate employer ships or reimburses required equipment; it does not ask for a kit fee, a training fee, or a deposit refunded out of a check you’re told to cash. That exact pattern — a check to deposit, then a request to send part of it back — is the single most common scam mechanism in Federal Trade Commission reports, and it shows up disguised as an “equipment” or “software license” requirement as often as anything else.
