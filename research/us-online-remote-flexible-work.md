# How people in the United States look for online, remote, and flexible work

Research brief compiled 2 November 2026 (Asia/Kuala_Lumpur).

The reader-facing pages are in `site/`, published as Paid Day. They are a public record of the distance between U.S. labor counts and the ad market: paid days, people in a survey week, and postings. They are not a job-search guide, they do not list jobs, and they do not take applications. This file remains the source note, with the survey limits and links.

This note answers two questions:

1. Where do people in the United States actually look when they want work they can do online, from home, or on a flexible schedule?
2. What do they expect that work to look like: the pay, the hours, the employment status, and the signs that an offer is real?

The short answer: search still starts on the big boards, especially Indeed, then LinkedIn, Google, and the employer’s own careers page. Fully remote jobs are a small share of ads and a large share of applications, so those ads are crowded. Most paid days are still worked on site. What people ask for, when the job cannot move home, is a schedule they can see coming and some say over the hours. Extra money is usually a small second stream. The median side hustle in the latest Bankrate survey is $200 a month, and about 5.5% of employed people hold two jobs. A posting that hides the pay, hides the employer, or asks for money up front is treated as a scam, because that is how the current wave of job fraud works.

The surveys below cover different groups: all adults, people at work in a reference week, remote-capable employees, job seekers, and side-hustle earners. Each figure is tied to its source so they are not treated as one sample.

## Three requests that get lumped together

“Online,” “remote,” and “flexible” are not the same offer in the U.S. market.

- **Remote or hybrid main job.** The work can be done from home some or all days. This is concentrated in jobs that are already computer-based: information, finance, professional services, insurance, legal, software, and some licensed counseling. Retail, driving, construction, food service, and most health-care bedside work are still on site.
- **Schedule flexibility.** The person still goes somewhere, or the work is hourly, but they want to know the hours in advance and to turn a shift down. Parents, students, and caregivers ask for this more often than they ask to never leave the house.
- **A small online or gig stream beside a main job.** Resale, freelance tasks, delivery, tutoring, crafts. Most people who do this want the extra money kept small. A minority want it to become the main job.

A page or an ad that promises all three at once (“work from your phone, set your own hours, replace your income”) matches the wording of the scams documented below, not the way most real openings are filled.

## How common remote work is, depending on the measure

Three official or long-running series describe late 2026. They do not measure the same thing.

### Share of paid days worked from home

The Survey of Working Arrangements and Attitudes (Barrero, Bloom, and Davis), published through FRED, measures the percent of full paid working days done at home. The sample is U.S. workers who earned at least $10,000 in a prior year, reweighted to the Current Population Survey.

| Month | Paid days worked from home |
| --- | --- |
| December 2019 | 7.2% |
| May 2020 | 61.7% |
| December 2022 | 29.3% |
| December 2024 | 26.9% |
| January 2026 | 24.5% |
| September 2026 | 26.8% |

The rate has sat in the mid-20s since 2023. It is about four times the 2019 level and less than half the spring 2020 peak.

Among full-time wage-and-salary workers in August 2026, the same project reports:

| Arrangement | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Fully on site | 60.8% |
| Hybrid | 27.1% |
| Fully remote | 12.1% |

In August 2026, women worked 29.3% of paid days from home and men 25.2%. For 2026 as a whole, among wage-and-salary employees, the home share of paid days was about 42% in information, 41% in finance and insurance, and 36% in professional and business services. It was about 20% in government, 19% in education, 15% in manufacturing, and 14% in transportation and warehousing.

Source: [FRED, Work from Home Rate (SWAA)](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/WFHCOVIDMATQUESTION); [FRED release, SWAA series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/release?rid=1033). Cite Barrero, Bloom, and Davis (2021), NBER Working Paper 28731, when using the series.

Education, for people 25 and older, is the sharp split:

| Education | Any telework in the week |
| --- | --- |

About 34.7 million people teleworked at least some hours that week, out of 158.5 million at work. A person with a bachelor’s degree is several times more likely to have done so than a person whose highest credential is a high school diploma. The American Time Use Survey’s 2025 annual averages tell a similar education story on days worked: 51.4% of employed people with a bachelor’s degree or higher worked at home on days they worked, against 19.0% of high school graduates with no college and 9.1% of people without a high school diploma. That home time includes unscheduled work, so it runs higher than the CPS “teleworked for pay” question.

### What remote-capable employees prefer

Gallup’s figures cover employees whose jobs could be done remotely at least some of the time. They are not a picture of the whole workforce. Remote-capable jobs are about half of U.S. employees in Gallup’s framing.

As of August 2026, among remote-capable employees:

| Where they work | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Hybrid (at least 10% and under 100% remote) | 54% |
| Exclusively remote | 25% |
| On site (under 10% remote) | 21% |

Asked in November 2025 what they would prefer: 61% hybrid, 33% exclusively remote, 6% on site. Hybrid is close to what they already have. Fully remote is a bit scarcer than they want. Fully on site is much more common than they want.

In May 2025, remote-capable Gen Z employees were the least likely generation to want fully remote work (23%, against 35% in each older generation) and the most likely to want hybrid (71%). Millennials were the most likely to say they would look for another job if remote options were taken away.

Sources: [Gallup, Hybrid Work indicator](https://www.gallup.com/401384/indicator-hybrid-work.aspx); [Gallup, fully remote work least popular with Gen Z](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/692675/fully-remote-work-least-popular-gen-z.aspx).

## Where the search actually happens

People use several channels at once. Two 2025 surveys ask different questions, and both put general boards first.

iHire’s 2025 report surveyed 1,421 U.S. job seekers. “Which resources do you use?” was select-all:

| Resource | Share of job seekers |
| --- | --- |
| General job boards | 78.5% |
| Professional networks such as LinkedIn | 54.9% |
| Industry or niche boards | 53.8% |
| Websites of companies they want | 45.0% |
| Search engines such as Google | 41.6% |
| Networking, referrals, word of mouth | 32.7% |
| Social media | 15.7% |
| Professional associations | 15.4% |
| Job fairs | 12.7% |
| Online classifieds such as Craigslist | 8.3% |
| Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT | 6.9% |
| Alumni or school career centers | 6.8% |
| Print ads | 4.7% |

HireClix’s 2025 candidate survey (1,000 U.S. adults 18 and older) asked where a search starts. Indeed 56%, LinkedIn 52% (up from 40% in their 2023 wave), Google 41%, corporate career sites 40%. Career sites were among the most trusted sources, up 42% since 2023 on their measure. Fifty-seven percent said reviews on Glassdoor or Indeed affect whether they apply.

A practical search for a professional role is Indeed or LinkedIn, then the employer’s own page, then a review site. A practical search for hourly, local, or shift work still uses Indeed, and adds Facebook groups, Craigslist, and sometimes a staffing agency. Niche boards matter once the person knows the field: USAJobs for federal roles, Dice or Wellfound for some technology roles, state job banks, and paid remote boards such as FlexJobs for people who will pay to filter. This brief does not have a current traffic-share ranking of those niche boards.

Social media is a research step more than an application step. HireClix found that more than half of Gen Z and millennial candidates check a company’s social accounts before applying, and that YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok job-ad engagement grew by double digits year over year. iHire still has social media at 15.7% of seekers as a place they search.

Employers, in the same iHire survey (529 employers), lean on employee referrals (71.3%), their own career page (49.5%), LinkedIn (46.1%), and social media (43.5%). A large share of hiring never appears as a clean public ad.

Sources: [iHire, State of Online Recruiting 2025](https://www.ihire.com/resourcecenter/employer/pages/the-state-of-online-recruiting-2025); [HireClix, 2025 Candidate Experience Survey](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250909919992/en/HireClix-Research-Reveals-How-Job-Seekers-Navigate-a-Modern-Labor-Market).

### How a search tends to run

1. Search a title plus “remote,” a city, or “part time.” Salary and work arrangement are the filters that decide whether the ad is opened.
2. Leave if the pay, the employer name, or the location type is missing. Figures are in the expectations section below.
3. Check the company site and a review site before spending time on the form.
4. Apply where the form is short. iHire found 51.7% of seekers think 10 to 15 minutes is the right length for an online application.
5. Keep a second path for cash that is not a job ad: marketplace selling, delivery apps, task platforms, and a direct message from someone they already know.

Competition on the big boards is high, and the reply rate is low. Huntr’s 2025 report, based on applications tracked in its own product (about 599,000 applications, so a self-selected user base, not a population survey), found interview response rates of about 4.5% on Indeed, 3.1% on LinkedIn, and 2.8% on ZipRecruiter, with higher rates on smaller or more curated destinations such as Google for Jobs (11.3% in that dataset). Treat those rates as a signal from one applicant-tracking product, not as a national placement rate.

Source: [Huntr, 2025 Annual Job Search Trends](https://huntr.co/research/2025-annual-job-search-trends-report).

## Remote ads are scarce relative to the applications they draw

People who want a remote main job are competing for a thin slice of postings.

Indeed’s own count of postings that mention remote or hybrid work, or list the location as remote:

| When | Share of U.S. Indeed postings |
| --- | --- |
| January 2019 | 2.6% |
| February 2022 peak | 10.4% |
| 31 October 2024 | 7.8% |
| 31 January 2025 | 8.3% |

A secondary compilation of Indeed Hiring Lab’s remote tracker puts the share at 8.4% on 31 August 2026, against 8.2% a year earlier, and says the share has stayed between about 7.7% and 9.1% since the start of 2024. That August 2026 point is not taken from an Indeed page opened for this brief. The occupation split in the same compilation, as a 7-day average to 31 August 2026, is useful because it shows why an all-jobs average stays low:

| Occupation group | Postings mentioning remote or hybrid |
| --- | --- |
| Insurance | 39.7% |
| Legal | 32.9% |
| Mathematics | 32.4% |
| Software development | 30.8% |
| Marketing | 25.2% |
| Human resources | 20.8% |
| Construction | 3.9% |
| Retail | 1.9% |
| Driving | 1.2% |

LinkedIn, which is weighted toward professional roles, shows the demand gap directly. A LinkedIn spokesperson told Business Insider that in September 2025 about 8% of paid U.S. job postings offered remote work and those postings drew 35% of applications. In July 2025 the company figure was about 9% of paid postings and 37% of applications. Remote roles are on the order of four times as applied-to as their share of the board.

Robert Half’s professional-job tracker, as reported in the same secondary compilation, is a different cut: finance, technology, marketing, legal, HR, admin, and healthcare support. It described fully on-site roles rising from 65% of the postings it tracks in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 87% in the second quarter of 2026, with 10% hybrid and 3% fully remote. Thirty-six percent of employers in its survey said they had raised required on-site days over the prior year. That sample is professional titles, so it should not be read as “87% of all U.S. jobs.”

The labor market around these ads is not a boom. BLS, seasonally adjusted, September 2026: unemployment 4.2% (7.1 million people), labor force participation 61.8%, employment-population ratio 59.2%. Payrolls rose 29,000 that month. Part time for economic reasons was 4.5 million. About 1.9 million people had been unemployed 27 weeks or more. Indeed’s Job Postings Index fell from 111.7 on 1 January 2025 to 101.7 at the end of October 2025, barely above the pre-pandemic baseline. Remote seekers are applying into a cooler market, onto a smaller set of ads.

Sources: [Indeed Hiring Lab, November 2024 labor market update](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2024/11/19/november-labor-market-update-remote-work/); [Forbes, 20 March 2025, citing Indeed](https://www.forbes.com/sites/kimelsesser/2025/03/20/remote-job-listings-fall-by-20-as-gender-pay-gap-remains-stagnant-indeed-finds/); [Second Talent compilation of the Indeed tracker and Robert Half, used here only for the August 2026 level and the occupation split](https://www.secondtalent.com/resources/remote-work-hiring-statistics/); [Business Insider, 19 October 2025, LinkedIn spokesperson](https://www.businessinsider.com/remote-companies-say-getting-flooded-with-applications-2025-10); [Los Angeles Times, 8 September 2025](https://www.latimes.com/business/story/2025-09-08/facing-stiff-competition-remote-workers-up-their-game); [BLS Employment Situation, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm); [BLS table A](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.a.htm); [Indeed Hiring Lab, 2026 U.S. jobs and hiring trends](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2025/11/20/indeed-2026-us-jobs-hiring-trends-report/).

Employers have also pulled back on formal flexibility benefits, without dropping them. SHRM’s 2026 Employee Benefits Survey of employers: 57% offer hybrid work, down 3 points from 2025, and 45% offer flextime during core business hours, down 6 points. Ten percent offer a four-day week, up 2 points. The share of employers who rate flexible-work benefits as very or extremely important was 66%, down 2 points from 68% in 2025. Among employers who subsidize at-home equipment, the maximum was about $901 a year, and 53% offered a subsidy at all.

Source: [SHRM, 2026 Employee Benefits Survey executive summary](https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/topics-tools/research/employee-benefits/2026_employee-benefits_executive-summary.pdf).

Federal civilian work is a special case. Gallup reported that after the 2025 federal return-to-office, hybrid among federal employees fell from 61% in late 2024 to 28% in the quarter that article covers, and 46% were fully on site. Federal job search belongs on USAJobs, and “remote federal job” is a much thinner promise than it was in 2023.

Source: [Gallup, Hybrid Work in Retreat? Barely.](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/694361/hybrid-work-retreat-barely.aspx)

## What “flexible” means when the job is not remote

The American Job Quality Study (Jobs for the Future and Gallup) surveyed 18,429 U.S. adults ages 18 to 75 who had worked for pay in the prior seven days, from 13 January to 25 February 2025.

A high-quality schedule, in that study, means the person knows the hours at least two weeks ahead unless they set their own, hours do not swing by more than 25% in a month unless they chose that, and they have some control. Sixty-two percent of employees did not have that. About 27% faced unpredictable schedules, 28% unstable schedules, and 41% had little or no control over their hours. Fifty-four percent often or sometimes worked longer than planned.

That is the gap a “flexible” hourly offer is walking into. People are not only asking to work from a laptop. They are asking to know the shift before they arrange childcare, school, or a second job, and to not have the week rewritten after they have planned it.

A vendor survey of the North American hourly workforce (Legion, 2025, not a BLS sample and not U.S.-only) found flexibility named as the top non-pay priority by 61% of hourly workers in that panel. Among those with more than one job, 82% said the reason was financial stability and 59% said one job did not cover rent or food. The multiple-job rate in that panel is higher than the national rate below, which fits an hourly sample. Use it as a description of what hourly workers say they are missing, not as a headcount.

Sources: [American Job Quality Study, 2025](https://www.jff.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/AmericanJobQualityReport%5FFinal%5F10142025.pdf); [Gallup write-up](https://news.gallup.com/poll/690881/work-schedules-fail-millions-employees.aspx).

### Side hustles

Bankrate / YouGov, 2–4 June 2025, 2,616 U.S. adults, of whom 699 had a side hustle. A side hustle was defined as extra money beyond the main source of income, so this is “I have one now,” not “I earned a dollar at some point this year.”

| | Share |
| --- | --- |
| U.S. adults with a side hustle | 27% |
| Same measure, 2024 | 36% |
| Same measure, 2023 | 39% |
| Gen Z (ages 18–28 in their banding) | 34% |
| Millennials (29–44) | 31% |
| Gen X (45–60) | 23% |
| Boomers (61–79) | 22% |

Income is skewed. The average is $885 a month and the median is $200. Fifty-eight percent earn more than $100 a month, 25% more than $500, and 14% more than $1,000. Twenty-eight percent earn $1 to $50. Most of this activity would not replace a paycheck.

What they do with at least some of the money: discretionary spending 41%, regular living expenses 35%, saving 28%, debt 20%. Twenty-nine percent think they will always need a side hustle to make ends meet. Sixteen percent want it to become their main income. Among Gen Z side hustlers that last share is 21%.

Online selling and skilled freelance work outrank rideshare. Delivery is real and visible, and it is not the typical side hustle.

QuickBooks’ December 2025 survey of 3,000 U.S. adults asked a wider question: any side-hustle income in the past 12 months. Forty-seven percent said yes (56% of Gen Z, 54% of millennials). Among those earners, 30% sold online and 22% freelanced. They reported about $2,038 a month and about 19.5 hours, which the write-up converts to roughly $104 an hour. That hourly figure is a ratio of two self-reports. It is not a wage an employer pays, and it is not comparable to the BLS average of $37.81. The same survey says side hustlers would need about $94,000 a year before they would quit a full-time job, and fewer than 11% had made that much from the side work. Forty percent of adults said they planned to start a business or side hustle in 2026 to build wealth.

Read the two surveys together. Bankrate says just over a quarter currently have a side stream, usually small, and the share fell in 2025. QuickBooks says nearly half earned something extra at least once in the year, younger adults more than older adults, and almost nobody is earning a replacement income from it. The motive mix is both bills and discretionary spending, not only desperation and not only a hobby.

Sources: [Bankrate press release, 9 July 2025](https://www.bankrate.com/press-releases/fewer-americans-have-a-side-hustle-in-2025/); [Bankrate survey write-up](https://www.bankrate.com/loans/small-business/side-hustles-survey/); [Intuit QuickBooks, State of Side Hustles](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/r/starting-a-business/state-of-side-hustles/).

## The pay they measure an offer against

Federal minimum wage is still $7.25 an hour, unchanged since 24 July 2009. Employers must pay the highest of federal, state, and local rates. The Department of Labor’s consolidated table dated 1 July 2026 lists 30 states plus the District of Columbia, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands above $7.25, 13 states at $7.25, and 7 states with no state minimum or a state minimum below $7.25 (the federal rate still applies where the Fair Labor Standards Act covers the job).

Examples from that 1 July 2026 table:

| Place | Hourly minimum on the 1 July 2026 table |
| --- | --- |
| District of Columbia | $18.40 |
| Washington | $17.13 |
| New York City and Long Island | $17.00 |
| California | $16.90 |
| Connecticut | $16.94 |
| New York, upstate | $16.00 |
| Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Missouri, Nebraska, Delaware | $15.00 |
| Florida | $14.00 |
| Texas, Pennsylvania, and others in the federal column | $7.25 |

A note on the same DOL page still describes the District of Columbia as $17.50. Use the dated table row and confirm with the District before putting a figure in consumer copy. A State Department notice said Florida’s minimum would rise to $15.00 on 30 September 2026. The July DOL table predates that step. Local rates in cities such as Seattle, New York City, and parts of California sit above the state figure and are not in the table.

BLS average earnings are far above the minimum, and they are an average, not a typical offer for entry-level flexible work. September 2026, seasonally adjusted, all private employees, preliminary: $37.81 an hour, $1,300.66 a week, 34.4 hours. Retail trade averaged $26.38 an hour and $807 a week. Leisure and hospitality averaged $23.90 an hour and $609 a week. A “flexible” offer near the federal minimum, with unpaid waiting time, unpaid training, or the worker covering their own fuel and mileage, reads as not worth it against those averages and against state floors of $15 and up.

Sources: [DOL, minimum wage](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/minimum-wage); [DOL consolidated table, effective 1 July 2026](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/mw-consolidated); [State Department OFM notice on 2026 increases](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/26-163-Notice-Minimum-Wage-Increases-in-2026.pdf); [BLS table B-3, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.t19.htm).

## What they expect before they will apply

iHire, same 1,421 job seekers. What would get them to apply (select all):

| Ask | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Say when the employer will be in touch | 60.5% |
| State the salary range | 57.1% |
| Name a hiring manager or recruiter | 40.7% |
| Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves | 36.2% |
| Spell out the duties | 35.7% |
| Say whether it is on site, remote, or hybrid | 31.9% |
| Shorten the application | 31.2% |
| List benefits | 27.2% |

Instant turnoffs: no salary 60.7%, anonymous or unnamed employer 50.9%, too many must-haves 37.2%, unclear or cute job titles 28.8%, misspellings 28.6%.

The hardest parts of the search, in that sample: applying and hearing nothing (59.0%), fake or already-filled “ghost” jobs (39.3% in the findings chart; the report’s summary rounds the same item to 39.9%), and finding a job that meets their pay (36.5%). Finding remote work was a stated challenge for 24.0%. Ageism (48.2%) was the trend they most expected to hurt them in the coming year, ahead of ghosting (47.4%) and fear of a weaker economy (46.0%).

Eighty-eight percent of that seeker sample were actively looking. The reasons were mixed: laid off 22.0%, unhappy in the role 17.6%, pay 15.6%, no way to advance 13.4%. “Want remote work” showed up in the write-in “other” bucket, not as a leading closed-ended reason. Pay, a layoff, and a bad job push the search. Remote is a filter they apply while searching, and a frustration when they cannot find it, more than it is the sentence they use for why they started.

Source: [iHire, State of Online Recruiting 2025](https://www.ihire.com/resourcecenter/employer/pages/the-state-of-online-recruiting-2025).

## Trust is part of the product

Job fraud is now a standard part of the remote-work search, which is why unnamed employers and upfront fees stop applications.

Federal Trade Commission, Consumer Sentinel:

- Reports of job and employment-agency scams tripled from 2020 to 2024, and reported losses in that subcategory rose from $90 million to $501 million (FTC press release, 10 March 2025).
- A later FTC note, September 2025, put 2024 job-scam reports above 105,000 and reported losses above $513 million.
- Business and job-opportunity fraud, a wider bucket, cost a reported $750.6 million in 2024.
- Task scams (repetitive online “jobs” such as liking videos or rating products, then a demand to deposit money to withdraw earnings) were about 20,000 reports in the first half of 2024, against about 5,000 in all of 2023, and nearly 40% of job-scam reports in that half. Reported job-scam losses had already passed $220 million in the first half of 2024. Crypto was the payment method with the largest reported losses.

The FTC’s consumer guidance describes the patterns people have learned to refuse:

- A text, WhatsApp, or Telegram message from a “recruiter,” often naming a known company, offering an “online assessor” or a generic remote role, sometimes asking only for a reply of “YES.”
- Pay that is specific (a daily or weekly rate) and duties that are not.
- Any fee to get the job, the training, the kit, or the first paycheck.
- A check to deposit, followed by a request to send part of it back or to buy equipment. The check is fake. The bank pursues the victim for the amount.
- Reshipping packages. That is not a job. It is moving goods bought with stolen cards.
- Being paid to like, rate, or “boost” anything, then being told to deposit money to unlock the balance.

The Better Business Bureau’s 2026 employment-scam update says nearly 50,000 people reported an employment scam to BBB Scam Tracker over three years, and that reports doubled in 2025 versus the year before. Median reported loss on the task scams in that tracker was $2,300 in 2025. BBB’s list of tells matches the FTC’s: unprompted offers, pay that is too high for the described work, pressure to start immediately, an interviewer who will not use a camera, pay for likes or views, and a fee or “tax” to withdraw money already “earned.”

Sources: [FTC, 10 March 2025 fraud losses](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/03/new-ftc-data-show-big-jump-reported-losses-fraud-125-billion-2024); [FTC, September 2025, job-scam counts](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2025/09/ftc-takes-steps-stop-deceptive-unfair-labor-market-practices); [FTC data spotlight on task scams, December 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2024/12/paying-get-paid-gamified-job-scams-drive-record-losses); [FTC, job scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams); [BBB, employment scams 2026 update](https://www.bbb.org/all/scamstudies/jobscams/employment-scams-2026-update).

## Employee or contractor is part of what they are judging

A large share of “online” and “flexible” offers are written as independent-contractor work: delivery platforms, freelance marketplaces, and some remote customer-support or annotation contracts. People comparing those offers with a W-2 job are comparing health coverage, overtime, unemployment insurance, and who pays the employer share of Social Security, not only the hourly rate.

Federal classification is in motion and is not something to paraphrase into ad copy. On 26 February 2026 the Department of Labor proposed to rescind its 2024 independent-contractor rule and replace it with a modified version of the 2021 economic-reality test. That test treats two factors as core: how much control the worker has over the work, and whether the worker has a real opportunity for profit or loss. Skill, permanence, and whether the work is integrated into the business are secondary. The comment period closed 28 April 2026. On 28 October 2026 the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs finished review of a final rule (“consistent with change”). As of 2 November 2026 that final rule had no publication date on the OIRA record. Until it is published, the 2024 rule remains the Department’s published guidance, and courts apply their own economic-reality case law. State tests are separate. California’s ABC test and similar state rules still decide unemployment, wage, and benefit coverage for many gig offers regardless of the federal rule.

What seekers already understand, without reading the Federal Register: if the company sets the hours, the script, and the rate, and the person cannot build a book of other clients, calling it a contract does not make it self-employment in their eyes. If the offer is truly a contract, they expect that stated in plain language, with who pays taxes and who supplies equipment.

Sources: [DOL proposal, 26 February 2026](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/whd/whd20260226); [Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/27/2026-03962/employee-or-independent-contractor-status-under-the-fair-labor-standards-act-family-and-medical); [OIRA conclusion, RIN 1235-AA46, 28 October 2026](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eoDetails?rrid=1546211). This section is a status note, not legal advice.

## What they expect, stated as conditions

Taken from the figures above, a U.S. offer that matches how people already search and what they refuse:

1. **Name the company.** Half of seekers treat an anonymous ad as a reason not to apply, and anonymity is a standard scam pattern.
2. **Show the pay as a range in dollars per hour or per year.** Say whether it is W-2 or 1099. In states with posting laws, the range is a legal requirement once the employer is over the size threshold. Everywhere else, missing pay is still the top turnoff.
3. **Say where the work happens.** On site, hybrid with the number of days, or fully remote. If it is remote, say whether it is open to every state or only some. Multi-state tax and registration are the employer’s problem to name, not a surprise after hire.
4. **Say when the hours are.** Fixed schedule, shifts posted two weeks out, or truly self-set. “Flexible” alone does not tell a parent or a second-job holder whether they can accept.
5. **Say what the work is in one concrete paragraph.** Vague titles (“online assessor,” “payment processing,” “product boosting”) are the vocabulary of current fraud.
6. **No payment in either direction before work.** No kit fee, no training fee, no equipment check, no crypto deposit, no “tax” to withdraw earnings. Equipment, if required, is shipped by the employer or reimbursed against a real invoice, not a check the person is told to cash and partially return.
7. **A person and a clock.** A named recruiter or hiring manager, and a statement of when applicants hear back. Silence is the most common complaint in the search.
8. **A short application.** About half of seekers put the acceptable form at 10 to 15 minutes. Re-typing a résumé they already uploaded is a stated complaint.
9. **A second-income offer should look like a second income.** The median current side hustle is $200 a month. Promising a replacement salary for a few hours of phone work is the sentence the FTC tells people is almost certainly a scam.
10. **Match the channel to the work.** Professional remote roles are found on LinkedIn, Indeed, and the company site, and they are crowded. Hourly flexible roles are found on Indeed, local groups, and staffing firms, and the decision turns on the shift and the rate. One ad written for both will be trusted by neither.

## Work that matches this market

These are the roles that show up when the search is actually for online, remote, or flexible work. Pay below is the May 2025 national median for the occupation in every setting, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, unless a figure is marked as a live ad. A live ad is one posting. It is not a promise that the same rate is open nationwide, and these ads expire.

### Remote jobs a high school diploma can reach

This is where the volume is. FlexJobs’ count of its own listings from 1 January to 1 May 2026 put customer service, administrative work, and sales at the top of entry-level remote hiring. Each of those three grew more than 30% in entry-level remote postings versus the year before. The titles posted most often were sales development representative, customer service representative, and business development representative, followed by account executive, financial analyst, staff accountant, administrative assistant, software engineer, registered nurse, and project coordinator.

The employers with the most entry-level remote postings in that database were Mass General Brigham, Thermo Fisher Scientific, HCA, the State of North Carolina, T-Mobile, IQVIA, Stride, Raymond James, Centene, and Toast.

Customer service is the occupation underneath most of those ads. BLS, May 2025: median $21.53 an hour ($44,770 a year). The lowest 10% earned under $15.27 and the highest 10% earned over $30.57. Employment is projected to fall 5% from 2025 to 2035, and about 289,500 openings a year still come from people leaving the occupation.

## How to read the numbers

- SWAA’s 26.8% is a share of paid days, mostly among people with enough earnings to be in that survey. It is the right figure for “how much of the country’s paid work happens at home.”
- BLS’s 21.9% is a share of people who worked in one September week and did any paid home work. It is the right figure for “how many workers teleworked at least a little,” and the education table under it is the right figure for who.
- Gallup’s 54% hybrid is among remote-capable employees only. It is the right figure for “what office workers already do,” and the wrong figure for retail, warehousing, or food service.
- Indeed’s roughly 8% is a share of ads, not of workers. LinkedIn’s 8% of ads drawing about 35% of applications is the right figure for how crowded a remote posting is.
- Bankrate’s 27% and QuickBooks’ 47% can both be true. One asks who has a side hustle now. The other asks who earned any extra income in a year. Neither is the BLS multiple-jobholding rate of 5.5%.
- Averages ($37.81 an hour, $885 a month from a side hustle) sit well above the medians and the minimums that entry-level offers are judged against. Use the median, or the state minimum, when describing a typical extra-income offer.
